# Long-running AI jobs: the waiting is the hard part

## Intended audience
Engineers and architects building LLM features that take minutes or hours
rather than seconds, and the leads who review those designs. Takeaway:
long-running AI work is a queueing and reliability problem first, and two
old rules (idempotency, durable is not correct) still decide whether it works.

## Body
```text
The hard part of our AI feature wasn't the model. It was the waiting.

At Centrica I worked on a lead discovery tool where a single search could run anywhere from 10 minutes to an hour. Crawl a company's website, embed it, index it, then ask the model questions against it.

You can't hold a request open for an hour. So searches went onto a queue, ran up to 10 at a time, and people got an email when theirs was done.

This week I read Kiran Prakash's article on durable execution for long-running agents on martinfowler.com. Two lines from it I'd pin above any desk building this kind of thing:

Retries give you at-least-once, not exactly-once. Anything with a side effect needs an idempotency key. {{confirm: did a retry or restart ever cause a duplicate for you, like a second email or a repeated crawl? One sentence on what happened}}

Durable is not the same as correct. A job that survives a crash can still finish with an answer that went stale while it waited.

None of it is new. Queues, logs, replay. We've had them for years. Agents just make the jobs longer and the steps harder to predict.

How long does your longest AI job run, and what happens when it dies halfway?
```
